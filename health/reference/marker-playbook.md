# Marker Playbook

Per marker: what it actually measures, what moves it, and the food-first
correction. Optimal ranges follow functional/longevity targets, which are
tighter than standard lab reference ranges — a standard range is built from the
population average, not from health.

---

## Energy & Metabolism

### Cystatin C — kidney filtration
**Optimal 0.55–0.9 mg/L.** A small protein produced at a constant rate by all
nucleated cells and cleared by the kidneys. Because production is steady, blood
level tracks filtration rate. Unlike creatinine it is *not* affected by muscle
mass, which makes it the better filtration marker in small, lean, elderly, or
very muscular people.

**Raised by, independent of kidney function:** hypothyroidism, corticosteroid
use, systemic inflammation, obesity, smoking, dehydration at draw.

**Interpretation rule:** a raised cystatin C with a *normal* creatinine points
toward those non-renal causes; a raised cystatin C *with* a raised creatinine is
a much stronger signal of true filtration loss. **Never call kidney disease off a
single cystatin C.** Confirm with creatinine, BUN, electrolytes, urine
albumin:creatinine ratio, and a repeat draw well-hydrated.

**Support (general kidney-protective, not a treatment):** adequate hydration,
blood pressure control, avoid chronic NSAIDs, moderate rather than extreme
protein, keep glucose optimal. Traditional: *Punarnava* (Ayurvedic kidney herb),
nettle leaf infusion, cordyceps (TCM Kidney tonic). **Do not self-treat a kidney
signal with herbs — many botanicals are cleared renally and some are nephrotoxic.
This one goes to a physician.**

### TSH — thyroid stimulating hormone
**Optimal 1–2 µIU/mL.** Not a thyroid hormone — it is the pituitary's *demand
signal*. It rises when the pituitary must shout louder at an underperforming
gland, so it moves before T4 and T3 leave range.

**Above 2.0–2.5 with symptoms = early under-function.** Confirm with free T3,
free T4, reverse T3, and TPO/thyroglobulin antibodies (antibodies distinguish
Hashimoto's autoimmunity from a simple nutrient shortfall — a different problem
with a different fix).

**Raw materials:** iodine, tyrosine, iron (TPO is a heme enzyme), selenium, zinc,
vitamin A. Fix ferritin first if it is low — see the iron-thyroid link.

**Food:** Brazil nuts (2/day, selenium), nori/wakame (iodine), oysters and
pumpkin seeds (zinc), liver (vitamin A), complete protein (tyrosine). Cook
brassicas rather than eating them raw in volume.

### Hemoglobin A1C and estimated average glucose
**Optimal 3.8–5.0% / eAG 70–97 mg/dL.** A1c is the percentage of hemoglobin
glycated by glucose — a ~3 month blood sugar average.

**Confounder that matters:** A1c depends on red blood cell lifespan. **Iron
deficiency falsely *raises* A1c** (older cells accumulate more glucose), so a low
A1c measured alongside iron deficiency is genuinely, unambiguously good — likely
even better than it reads.

### Morning cortisol
**Optimal 10–15 µg/dL, drawn 7–9am.** The primary stress and wakefulness hormone.
Should peak 30–45 min after waking and taper all day.

**Check draw time before interpreting.** A value drawn at 10am is not comparable
to one drawn at 7:30am — this alone explains many "low" results.

Genuinely low: under-fueling, sleep debt, circadian disruption, overtraining,
chronic stress, or inadequate cholesterol substrate. **Very low with low sodium,
high potassium, and hyperpigmentation suggests Addison's disease — a medical
diagnosis requiring an ACTH stimulation test, not herbs.**

**Support:** morning sunlight within an hour of waking; protein-rich breakfast
within 60–90 min; adequate salt; consistent sleep and wake times; strength and
walking over chronic hard cardio; ashwagandha; holy basil; bone broth.

### Triglyceride:HDL ratio
**Optimal under 1.1.** One of the best single proxies for insulin sensitivity and
for LDL particle size. Under 1.5 is good; over 3 suggests insulin resistance and
a small-dense-LDL pattern. Moved by reducing refined carbohydrate and alcohol,
and by raising omega-3 intake.

## Heart Health & Cardiovascular Resilience

### ApoB — the number that matters most
**Optimal 50–70 mg/dL.** Every atherogenic particle carries exactly one ApoB
molecule, so ApoB is a direct *particle count*. Particle count predicts
cardiovascular risk better than LDL-C, because it is particles — not the
cholesterol inside them — that lodge in the artery wall. If ApoB is optimal,
cardiovascular risk is genuinely low regardless of how the other lipids look.

### LDL-C:ApoB ratio — particle size
**Optimal 1.5–2.0.** Divides cholesterol *content* by particle *count*, which
estimates how much cholesterol each particle carries — i.e. particle size.
- **Low ratio (<1.2):** cholesterol-poor particles = **small, dense LDL
  (Pattern B)**, which penetrates the artery wall more readily.
- **High ratio:** large, buoyant, cholesterol-rich particles (Pattern A).

**Important caveat:** when LDL-C is *calculated* rather than directly measured,
the Friedewald equation loses accuracy at low cholesterol and low triglyceride
values, and can understate LDL-C — which mechanically depresses this ratio.
Before treating a low ratio as a finding, confirm with a **direct LDL** or an
**NMR LipoProfile** particle-size panel.

Genuine small-dense-LDL drivers: insulin resistance, high triglycerides, low
thyroid, inflammation. Correct with omega-3s, reduced refined carbohydrate,
resistance training, and thyroid sufficiency.

### HDL cholesterol
**Optimal 80–100 mg/dL; under 50 is low for a woman.** Reverse cholesterol
transport — moves cholesterol back out of tissues. Function matters more than
number, but very low HDL is still an unfavorable signal.

**Raises HDL:** extra-virgin olive oil (2–4 tbsp/day), fatty fish and omega-3,
pastured egg yolks, avocado, nuts, aerobic exercise (zone 2), resistance
training, and reducing refined carbohydrate. Trans fats lower it sharply.

### Total cholesterol — when it is too LOW
**Optimal 160–200 mg/dL.** Modern medicine watches only the ceiling, but the
floor matters. Cholesterol is the raw substrate for:
- **every steroid hormone** — pregnenolone → progesterone, DHEA, cortisol,
  estrogen, testosterone
- **vitamin D** — skin 7-dehydrocholesterol + UVB → D3
- **bile acids** — which absorb the fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E, K
- **cell membranes and myelin** — roughly 25% of body cholesterol sits in the brain

Low total cholesterol has been associated with low mood and hormonal
insufficiency. Causes: under-eating or low fat intake, malabsorption (low bile,
low stomach acid, celiac), hyperthyroidism, liver under-function, and rarely
genetic hypobetalipoproteinemia.

**Raise with:** pastured egg yolks (2–3 daily), extra-virgin olive oil, butter or
ghee from grass-fed cows, avocado, coconut, nuts, fatty fish, full-fat dairy if
tolerated. Support bile with bitter greens and beets so the fat is actually
absorbed.

## Inflammation & Immune Defense

### hs-CRP
**Optimal under 1.0 mg/L.** Systemic inflammation. Under 1 is excellent and
broadly protective. Note it *also* validates a ferritin reading — because
ferritin rises with inflammation, a low ferritin measured next to a low CRP is
unambiguously true iron deficiency, not a masked value.

### Homocysteine
**Optimal 5–7 µmol/L.** An amino-acid intermediate cleared by B-vitamin-dependent
pathways. Above 7 signals a methylation shortfall; above 10–12 is associated with
cardiovascular and cognitive risk. Relevant to fertility and pregnancy.

**Nutrients:** folate (as 5-MTHF), B12 (methylcobalamin), B6 (P5P), B2
(riboflavin), betaine/TMG, magnesium.

**Food:** liver and leafy greens (folate), beets (betaine — the single best food
lever), shellfish and red meat and eggs (B12), pastured poultry and pistachios
(B6). Prefer methylated forms; folic acid is poorly converted in MTHFR variants.

**Loop back:** B12 absorption requires stomach acid and intrinsic factor. High
homocysteine alongside low iron implicates the stomach, not the diet.

## Nutrition & Building Blocks

### 25-OH Vitamin D
**Optimal 50–80 ng/mL.** Deficient under 20, insufficient under 30. A
prohormone acting on hundreds of genes — immune regulation, calcium and bone,
mood, hormones.

**Sun is the ancient and primary source:** midday, arms and legs exposed, 10–30
min depending on skin tone and latitude, before burning. Above ~37° latitude,
winter UVB is too weak to make any D at all — supplementation is not optional
there from roughly October to March.

**Food:** cod liver oil (traditional, and delivers A alongside D — they work as a
pair), wild salmon, sardines, mackerel, egg yolks, sun-dried mushrooms.

**Supplement:** D3 (cholecalciferol) 2,000–5,000 IU/day with a fat-containing
meal. **Always pair with vitamin K2 (MK-7, 100–200 µg)** — D increases calcium
absorption, and K2 directs that calcium into bone rather than arteries.
Magnesium is required to convert D into its active form; correcting magnesium is
often why a stalled D level finally moves. Retest at 8–12 weeks; do not exceed
10,000 IU/day without monitoring, as vitamin D is fat-soluble and accumulates.

### Ferritin
**Optimal 70–90 ng/mL for women; under 30 is deficient; under 50 is associated
with fatigue and hair shedding.** Stored iron — the savings account, where serum
iron is only the checking account. Stores empty long before hemoglobin falls,
which is why fatigue can precede anemia by months and why a "normal CBC" does not
rule out iron deficiency.

**Confirm with:** full iron panel (serum iron, TIBC, transferrin saturation), CBC
with MCV and RDW, and hs-CRP to validate. **Then find the cause:** menstrual blood
loss, celiac disease, H. pylori, GI bleeding, low stomach acid, blood donation,
or a diet low in heme iron.

**Heme iron (from animals) is absorbed at ~15–35%; non-heme (from plants) at only
~2–20%** and is heavily blocked by phytates, tannins, and calcium. This is why
plant-forward eaters need substantially more attention here.

**Food, with real numbers per serving:**
| Food | Serving | Iron |
|---|---|---|
| Canned oysters | 3 oz | ~6–8 mg (heme) |
| Beef liver | 3 oz | ~5 mg (heme) + B12, folate, retinol, copper |
| Clams | 3 oz | ~12–24 mg (heme) |
| Grass-fed beef | 6 oz | ~4 mg (heme) |
| Sardines | 3.75 oz tin | ~2.5 mg + omega-3 + D |
| Cooked lentils | 1 cup | ~6.6 mg (non-heme) |
| Pumpkin seeds | 1 oz | ~2.5 mg (non-heme) |
| Blackstrap molasses | 1 tbsp | ~1–2 mg (non-heme) |

**Absorption rules — worth as much as the supplement:**
- **Take with vitamin C.** Ascorbate converts ferric to absorbable ferrous iron
  and can multiply non-heme absorption several-fold. Amla, camu camu, bell
  pepper, citrus, or 250–500 mg ascorbic acid.
- **A small amount of meat raises absorption of the non-heme iron eaten with it**
  (the "meat factor") — the traditional pairing of lentils with a little meat.
- **Separate from blockers by 2 hours:** tea and coffee (tannins block up to
  60–90%), calcium and dairy, whole grains and legumes eaten unsoaked (phytate),
  spinach and chard (oxalate), zinc and magnesium supplements, antacids and PPIs.
- **Soak, sprout, or sour** grains and legumes to break down phytate. Sourdough
  fermentation does this; quick-rise bread does not.
- **Cook in bare cast iron** — acidic simmered foods pick up meaningful iron.

**Supplement — dose *timing* is the modern finding that matters:** oral iron
raises hepcidin, the hormone that shuts down iron absorption, for about 24 hours,
and it returns to baseline by 48. **So alternate-day dosing absorbs roughly twice
as much total iron as daily dosing, and a single morning dose beats split doses.**
More is genuinely worse.
- **Ferrous bisglycinate (chelated) 25–50 mg elemental, every other morning, on
  an empty stomach, with vitamin C.** Bisglycinate is far gentler than ferrous
  sulfate. Liquid herbal tonics (Floradix) are gentler still at lower potency.
- **Never supplement iron without confirmed deficiency.** Iron overload
  (hemochromatosis) is real, common, and damages liver, heart, and pancreas. Iron
  is also the leading cause of fatal poisoning in young children — store it locked.
- Ferritin rises slowly: retest at **8–12 weeks**, expecting roughly 10–30 ng/mL
  of movement per 3 months. Repletion from 20 to 70+ typically takes 6–12 months.

## Hormonal Communication

### Estradiol and FSH (premenopausal women)
**Both are meaningless without the cycle day.** Estradiol swings from ~30 pg/mL
in the early follicular phase to 200–400 at the ovulatory peak. FSH in the early
follicular phase is typically ~3–10 mIU/mL; a persistently elevated FSH above
~25 alongside a low estradiol suggests perimenopause or menopause.

Always record and report the cycle day. Reading these numbers without it is
guessing.
